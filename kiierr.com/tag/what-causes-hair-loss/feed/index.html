]]> Millions of men and women all over the world suffer from hair loss. Unlike normal hair shedding — where people lose 50-100 strands of hair each day — hair loss is unexpected and rapid. Hair loss patients shed more than 125 hairs per day, and not enough hairs grow back to restart the growth cycle. Aside from hair thinning, patients may see bald spots appear all over the scalp, or experience total full-body hair loss.

Some causes of sudden hair loss include certain medical conditions or medications, but one of the most prevalent causes is stress. When the body is stressed, your blood and energy are redirected towards vital organs like your lungs, heart, and brain; this means your scalp does not receive enough nutrients to support healthy hair growth. Unfortunately, chronic hair loss can lead to further mental stress and low self-esteem, which can begin a vicious cycle.

How Hair Loss Causes Mental Stress

Hair loss is not a life-threatening phenomenon, but it does have the potential to create psychological effects like stress, anxiety, depression, low self-esteem, antisocial personality disorder, post-traumatic disorder, and other emotional issues.

The problem is a cultural one. For centuries, a full head of hair has been a common measure of beauty, youth, and health around the world. Among men, hair is tied to ideas of strength and virility — like Samson in the Bible — while it is considered a sign of femininity among women. Moreover, hair is a prominent physical feature. When hair loss occurs, it’s often obvious so it becomes a source of embarrassment or shame.

This aspect of hair loss can trigger a psychological breakdown, as it creates an exaggerated feeling of ugliness and appearance-related anxieties. Many people who suffer from hair loss end up withdrawing from relationships and avoiding socialization. Their quality of life also suffers, and they end up neglecting to take care of themselves as a reaction to hair loss.

1. Build healthy habits

The good news is that mental stress and hair loss can both be mitigated by healthy habits. A balanced diet, coupled with exercise, can help reduce hair fall — while keeping your brain and body in shape. Foods like fatty fish and walnuts contain omega-3, which can help with hair regrowth and combat mood disorders.

Research on hair loss in women also recommends improving sleep quality and avoiding alcohol consumption to prevent hair loss from deteriorating further. Plus, these habits can help you maintain your well-being in the long run too.

2. Consult a psych professional

The mental stress and emotional impact of hair loss may prevent you from living your life; patients report psychosocial burdens such as helplessness, anger, frustration, and depression that lead to social isolation. In these cases, talking to a psychologist or psychiatrist may be the best course of action — and fortunately, many of them are more accessible today through telehealth arrangements.

Those with professional psychology credentials can help manage extreme stress since they’re trained in the biological, social, and clinical aspects of human behavior. This allows them to create a comprehensive mental and behavioral healthcare program for patients experiencing stress. Talking to a psych professional can help you understand your problems and devise ways to cope.

]]> Hair loss is a normal part of the hair cycle. Everyone from young children to elderly individuals lose hair. But for some people, normal hair loss turns into excessive hair loss. Do you know the difference?

How Hair Loss Works

Hair growth begins beneath the skin of the scalp inside the follicle. Hair is nourished by blood vessels that are connected to the base of the follicle. As time passes, each hair goes through a series of four stages that carry it from initial growth all the way through shedding. This process can last for several years, after which each follicle restarts the process and does it all over again. 

To understand hair loss, you must understand hair growth. The phases are as follows:

  • Growth Phase: This initial phase is the longest of the four. It can last for two to seven years. During this part of the process, hair grows to as long as 30 inches (if uncut). The length and speed of growth are highly dependent on factors like age, health, genetics, and nutrition.  
  • Transition Phase: Once the growth phase ends, the follicle enters into a transitory phase. This phase lasts for just a few weeks. During this concentrated period of time, hair stops growing and becomes detached from the blood supply. The hair becomes known as a “club” hair at this point. 
  • Resting Phase: During this part of the process – which lasts for roughly three months – new hair begins growing beneath the club hair. Eventually, the new hair pushes the club hair out. This leads to shedding. 

Typically, it takes several years for a follicle to go through all of these phases. However, certain factors – including genetic and lifestyle factors – may cause hair to enter the resting phase prematurely. This leads to excessive shedding and thinning. 

What is Normal Hair Loss?

Hair loss is a game of addition and subtraction. You’re always losing hair and you’re always growing hair. The net result is what’s important.

Believe it or not, it’s normal to lose as many as 100 hairs per day. The average person loses somewhere between 50 to 100 hairs daily (that’s as many as 1,500 to 3,000 hairs every month – or 18,000 to 36,000 per year). However, because the average human head has as many as 100,000 hairs at any given time, the loss of 100 hairs is just a drop in the bucket. At the end of the day, it’s not noticeable to the naked eye.

Problems occur when hair loss increases and/or hair growth slows down. When hair growth/loss turns from “net neutral” to “net negative,” thinning occurs. After several months, this thinning leads to balding. 

The question of how much hair loss is normal is highly dependent on how much hair growth is occurring. The more hair you grow – or the faster you grow it – the more hair loss you can sustain. Somewhere between 50 to 100 hairs is fairly normal. It all depends on your individual circumstances.

What to Do With Excessive Hair Loss

If you’re experiencing excessive hair loss – a.k.a. a “net negative” – there are a few things you can do to slow down the rate of loss, speed up the growth rate, and flip to a “net positive” state. 

  • Optimize Your Diet

Hair doesn’t grow with magic. It takes a careful blend of the right nutrients. Thus, the first step is to ensure you’re consuming a proper diet that’s high in protein, iron, biotin, omega-3 fatty acids, vitamin A, vitamin D, vitamin C, and zinc. You also need to stay hydrated throughout the day. In addition to consuming the right vitamins and nutrients, it’s important that you avoid the wrong foods. Stay away from highly-processed foods, refined sugar, and saturated fats as much as you possibly can. 

  • Stop Making These Hair Care Mistakes

Your hair care regimen has a direct impact on your hair health. The more intentional you are with caring for your hair, the lower your hair loss rate will be. Here are a few things to avoid:

  1. Don’t overwash. There’s no need to shampoo and wash your hair on a daily basis. In fact, if you’re washing your hair more than three times per week, there’s a pretty good chance that you’re doing more harm than good. Only wash as needed (and be as gentle as you possibly can).
  2. Don’t overheat. Heat styling tools, such as straighteners, curling irons, and blow dryers, are not kind to your hair. They make hair dry and brittle, both of which lead to an increased likelihood of hair loss. 
  3. Don’t overstyle. Tight hairstyles (like ponytails or cornrows) pull on hair and may lead to excessive hair loss. In many cases, it causes hair that’s still in the growth phase to fall out.

If you abide by these three hair care rules, you’ll limit damage to your hair and encourage healthy growth. 

  • Give Medication a Try

There are two commonly used medications for hair loss. Over-the-counter minoxidil (Rogaine) comes in a variety of forms, including liquid, foam, and shampoo. This medication is designed to slow the rate of hair loss and encourage steady regrowth. 

The second option is finasteride (Propecia). It requires a prescription and is taken in a pill form. Finasteride is primarily designed to stop excessive hair loss, though it can also encourage some new hair growth.

What Causes Hair Loss? The Most Common Causes

Heredity

According to the American Academy of Dermatology, or the AAD, the leading cause of hair loss in men and women is genetics. Men who suffer from male-pattern hair loss and women who suffer from female-pattern hair loss, also known as androgenic alopecia, have genes passed on to them that cause their hair follicles to shrink and stop growing hair over time. The old “rule” of following your maternal grandfather is largely a myth. This process can begin at several stages in life, but it generally hits hardest as people get older. 

Some people have inherited genes that enable their body to produce more dihydrotestosterone, or DHT, which is now understood to be one of the main causes of hair loss in both men and women. While changing your DNA is obviously not a feasible option, there are a number of products that block DHT and can help your hair grow back thicker and healthier. These include DHT blocking shampoo, Conditioner for Hair Growth, and even DHT blocking vitamins.

The Aging Process

A lot of things happen as we get older. Men lose testosterone. Women face menopause. We physically begin to experience physical declines in ways that are seemingly unavoidable. We can’t run as fast as we used to or jump as high, and we can’t function on little sleep like we did when we were younger. 

The same happens with hair loss, as the aging process includes a slowdown of the hair growth process. That leads to thinning hair, the loss of hair and the loss of color in the hair.

Cancer Treatments

According to the United States Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, or the CDC, approximately 650,000 people undergo chemotherapy in the United States every year. Hundreds of thousands more undergo radiation treatments, and these are just people in the United States. Aside from the obvious concern that we all have for those who suffer from some form of cancer given its potential outcome, it’s also difficult for many people to accept the fact that these treatments cause hair loss in the majority of the patients who are treated. 

Traumatic Events

When we talk about traumatic events, we’re not just referring to negative occurrences, which are obviously difficult for anyone to overcome. Traumatic events are those that substantially alter our lives, even temporarily. Clearly, the death of a loved one is traumatic, but so is childbirth. Any of these types of events can lead to a massive amount of stress, and the physical and chemical changes in the body during this time can and often do lead to some type of hair loss.

Infection

Almost every part of our body is on some level vulnerable to some type of infection. There are thousands of different infections that doctors can diagnose, and while treatment is often available, that doesn’t mean that the infection still doesn’t take its toll. The scalp is one of those areas of the body that can become infected in a number of ways. It’s common for people who suffer from a scalp infection to also experience hair loss on some level.

Medication Side Effects

There are medications that exist these days for just about any type of illness or other problem we can consider. Many of these medications, especially those that are available only via prescription, are extremely powerful and can carry significant side effects. One common side effect of different types of medication is the temporary loss of hair. In some cases, that hair loss can take months to overcome.

Psoriasis 

The skin is an organ. It exists on every outward location on the body to protect our organs and sensitive tissues. The skin, like anything else, is vulnerable to infections, and one of those that arises often is known as psoriasis. Psoriasis is a problem where skin cells tend to build up with too much density, and this buildup leads to patches of scaly, itchy skin. Psoriasis can and does occur on the scalp, and that can lead to hair loss. 

Thyroid Disease

The thyroid is a vital gland in the body that helps to regulate its systems by releasing hormones into the circulatory system with regularity. If something goes wrong with the thyroid, it can lead to a whole host of problems, as at that point the body’s allocation of hormones is knocked off balance. When this occurs, it can affect the hair follicles and the scalp among many other areas, and this in turn can lead to hair loss.

Vitamin Deficiencies

We need all different types of vitamins to remain healthy. We get most of them through the foods we consume, and if we go for prolonged periods of time without consuming the proper amount of certain types of vitamins, the body is going to react. For instance, if we don’t consume enough biotin, iron, protein or zinc, our hair can begin to thin and eventually fall out of our scalp.

Scarring Alopecia

When people have surgeries or they are injured in the scalp area, it often leaves scars behind. These scars, obviously, are permanent, and the damage inflicted on the scalp is often such that the hair follicles are permanently destroyed. This clearly leads to a loss of hair, and it’s extremely difficult for some and impossible for others to be able to regrow hair after this takes place.

Why Am I Losing My Hair?

The first question to consider with regards to hair regrowth is why you’re losing it in the first place. Generally speaking, hair follicles go through three phases in their life cycles, including:

  • The Anagen Phase – Your hair grows an average of 1 cm per month, and this phase generally lasts between two and six years.
  • The Catagen Phase – This is a transitional phase that lasts between two and three weeks. The follicle “renews itself” by being cut off from nourishment provided by blood and regrows.
  • The Telogen Phase – When a hair follicle goes dormant, it can last for up to four months. For most people, 10 – 15 percent of their hair is in this phase. When too many follicles transition into the Telogen Phase, it can lead to hair loss.

What Causes Hair Loss?

That leads to the obvious question of what causes hair loss? There are a number of factors associated with what is commonly known as male/female pattern baldness or androgenetic alopecia, but perhaps the most prominent is Dihydrotestosterone, or DHT. If you have too much DHT in your body, it can basically kill off your hair follicles and lead to baldness in both men and women. 

Types of Hair Loss

Everyone’s hair is different. From thick and log to thin and short, there is a wide spectrum of hair, and everyone’s hair grows a little differently. When it comes to hair loss, things can get equally complicated. While the spectrum for hair loss can be wide and varied as well, there are a few main categories that the types of hair loss generally fit into.

These types include:

  • Male/female Pattern Baldness – usually associated with a receding hairline, starting from the front and working its way back.
  • Alopecia Areata – an autoimmune condition that can catch patches of hair to stop growing. This can affect the hair on your head, as well as on areas of your body.
  • Telogen Effluvium – occurs when the hair follicles on the scalp enter the final stage of the hair growth cycle but don’t proceed to the next growth phase. As a result, thinning may be noticed in different areas of the head.
  • Anagen Effluvium – This type of hair loss is most associated with medical treatments like chemotherapy. Typically, the hair loss only occurs during the treatments, and the hair grows back when the treatment ends.
  • Scarring Alopecia – a rare type of hair loss that occurs due to inflammatory skin conditions and disorders that destroy hair follicles. The follicles can be replaced with scar tissue that stops the hair from growing back.
